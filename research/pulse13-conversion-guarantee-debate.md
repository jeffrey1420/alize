## Assumption 1: "50% Refund If Unsatisfied" Is a Sufficient Guarantee

### Why It May Be Wrong

The 50% refund guarantee (covering the pilot month only) solves for the wrong risk. It addresses post-pilot disappointment — but the real blocker is pre-pilot commitment. For an unknown brand selling to a French PME buyer, the question isn't "what if I'm unhappy?" It's "what if Alizé disappears mid-pilot?"

A 50% refund doesn't protect the client from:
- **Execution failure**: The agent doesn't work, Alizé can't fix it in 30 days, the client gets €2,250 back — but lost 30 days, internal disruption, and embarrassment.
- **Vendor abandonment**: Alizé shuts down or pivots. The refund clause is worthless if there's no one to collect from.
- **Timeline to refund**: French business owners are acutely aware that "money-back guarantees" often require fighting for the refund. The perceived collectability of a refund is low.

The guarantee also has a signaling problem: a guarantee is what you offer when you're worried about trust.显ly confident vendors don't lead with refund policies — they lead with track records.

### What to Do Differently

Replace the guarantee as a *trust mechanism* with a *confidence mechanism*:
1. **Require a signed exit criteria document** before the pilot starts — define success together in writing. This reframes the guarantee from "you can leave if unhappy" to "we've already agreed on what success looks like."
2. **Stage the pilot payment**: 50% at kickoff, 50% at day 15. If Alizé doesn't deliver working results by day 15, the client can exit without the second payment. This makes the exit automatic, not adversarial.
3. **Add a performance bond framing**: Alizé puts its own reputation on the line. If the agent doesn't achieve the agreed metrics within 30 days, Alizé delivers the next workflow at no additional setup cost.

### NEW Decision / Unresolved Question

**Decision:** Replace the 50% refund with a **staged payment + signed exit criteria** model. The refund language should appear only in the contract, never in sales materials — it signals weakness.

**Unresolved:** What is the minimum viable case study / social proof required before the guarantee mechanism even matters? A French PME buyer needs to believe Alizé exists before they care about the refund.

## Assumption 2: "Operational Ownership Transfer" Framing Compels DG/COO Sign-Off

### Why It May Be Wrong

"Operational ownership transfer" was adopted in Pulse 9 (D29) to make clients feel they've delegated entirely. The logic: if the client feels ownership transfer, they engage more deeply and sign off more readily.

But consider what "transfer" sounds like to a DG or COO who has spent 10-15 years building their operational playbook:

**The control anxiety problem.** "Ownership transfer" implies the DG is giving something away. In a 20-50 person company, operational control is the DG's primary source of authority. Saying you're transferring it creates an implicit anxiety: "What do I lose?" The DG doesn't want to delegate their job — they want to delegate a task while retaining oversight. "Transfer" is the wrong word.

**The accountability gap.** If Alizé "owns" the workflow operationally, who is accountable when it breaks? The DG, who is legally responsible for the business? Or Alizé, who says they own it? "Ownership transfer" creates ambiguous accountability — exactly the opposite of what a risk-averse PME buyer wants.

**The team displacement anxiety.** French DG/COOs are acutely aware of workforce implications. If Alizé "owns" an operational workflow, what does that mean for the two people currently doing that work? In a French context, this triggers both practical (what do I do with the team?) and political (works council implications, labor narrative) concerns. The framing may provoke resistance before the conversation even starts.

**"Delegate" outperforms "transfer".** Clients want to delegate a task and know it's handled. They don't want to transfer ownership — ownership implies responsibility they can't shake. "Delegation with guarantees" is the mental model that works.

### What to Do Differently

1. Replace "ownership transfer" language with **"delegation with accountability"** — Alizé operates the workflow, the client retains oversight and can intervene anytime.
2. Add explicit accountability language: **"If it breaks, we fix it. If it doesn't perform, we adjust. You always have visibility."**
3. Never frame the agent as replacing a person — frame it as handling the tasks that person doesn't have time for. The existing employee becomes the supervisor of the agent, not the replaced worker.

### NEW Decision / Unresolved Question

**Decision:** Replace "operational ownership transfer" with **"delegation with accountability"** in all DG/COO-facing materials. Test this framing with the first pilot client and measure whether it reduces objection volume around control.

**Unresolved:** Does the DG/COO framing need to differ from the Head of Operations framing? A COO may welcome ownership transfer language; a DG may resist it. Should there be two distinct conversion narratives?

## Assumption 3: Month-to-Month Signals Confidence (vs. Lock-In)

### Why It May Be Wrong

Month-to-month was adopted to reduce commitment friction — make it easy for the client to say yes. But consider what month-to-month says to a French PME buyer evaluating an unknown vendor:

**It signals Alizé doesn't trust itself to deliver.** Committed vendors ask for 6-12 month contracts. Vendors who know they'll lose clients after month 1 offer month-to-month. The inference is automatic and unflattering.

**It creates perverse incentives for Alizé.** Month-to-month means every month is a renewal conversation. This burns Alizé's energy on retention instead of delivery, and trains clients to evaluate Alizé monthly rather than quarterly.

**It doesn't match how French PME buyers think about vendor relationships.** French businesses have a well-documented preference for established, relational vendor models (the comptable, the expert-comptable, the ESN). These relationships are built on multi-year trust, not monthly随时解散. A vendor who wants to be treated as a trusted partner should act like one.

**The irony: lock-in can be a selling point.** For a €1,200-1,500/month service that requires deep integration (access to tools, documents, team workflows), clients want to know Alizé is committed to the relationship. A 12-month retainer with an exit clause after month 3 signals commitment. Month-to-month signals Alizé might walk away if things get hard.

### What to Do Differently

1. **Offer a 12-month retainer with a structured exit clause after month 3**: Client can exit with 30 days notice after month 3 if agreed metrics aren't met. This gives Alizé the stability to invest in the relationship while giving the client a genuine escape valve.
2. **Price the month-to-month option at a premium**: If you offer month-to-month, charge 20% more. This makes the 12-month commitment the default attractive option and frames it as the "confident vendor" choice.
3. **Never describe month-to-month as "no lock-in"**: Frame it as "flexibility after trust is established." The first 3 months are fixed; after that, you can leave with 30 days notice. This positions the flexibility as earned, not default.

### NEW Decision / Unresolved Question

**Decision:** Kill month-to-month as the default post-pilot structure. Replace with a **12-month retainer with a performance-based exit clause at month 3**. Month-to-month becomes the premium flexible option, not the standard.

**Unresolved:** Should Alizé ask for 3 months pre-paid (like a retainer model) rather than monthly invoicing? Pre-payment signals client commitment and improves Alizé's cash position — but it may increase friction at conversion. Test with first 2-3 pilots.
