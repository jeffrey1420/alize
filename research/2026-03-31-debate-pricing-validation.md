This isn't just my opinion. This is the foundation of Lean Startup methodology (Eric Ries), Customer Development (Steve Blank), and every empirical study of startup pricing validation in B2B contexts.

## What the Literature Actually Says

### 1. Lean Startup: Price is a Hypothesis, Not a Decision

Eric Ries establishes in *The Lean Startup* that everything is a hypothesis until tested with real customers. Pricing is not a planning exercise — it's a market experiment. The moment Louis quotes a price to a real prospect and observes their reaction, he gets data. Until then, he's operating on assumptions dressed up as strategy.

**Key principle:** Validated learning requires actual market contact. Internal debate is not a substitute.

### 2. Steve Blank: Get Out of the Building

Steve Blank's Customer Development model is explicit: you don't know what customers will pay until you ask them. Not in surveys. Not in interviews. In actual sales conversations with actual price quotes. The entire framework of "discovery, qualification, proposal, negotiation" assumes price is negotiable and discoverable through contact — not pre-determined.

**Key principle:** Pricing architecture is learned from the market, not derived from internal cost-plus calculations.

### 3. The Zappos Experiment

Nick Swinmurn famously tried to sell shoes online before Amazon existed. He had no inventory. He listed products on a website, took orders, then went to the mall to buy what customers ordered at retail. He was testing whether people would buy shoes online. **He was testing demand and willingness-to-pay simultaneously, before building any infrastructure.** This is the canonical example of "price discovery before product-market fit."

### 4. B2B Pricing Reality: Price Is Always Negotiable

In B2B, prices are not fixed labels. They're starting points for negotiation. The entire concept of "finalizing pricing architecture" before market contact misunderstands how B2B sales works. Every B2B deal is custom-priced through a discovery → proposal → negotiation → close cycle. Louis isn't setting a retail price; he's establishing a negotiating position.

**The assumption that pricing must be "finalized" treats B2B like B2C. It isn't.**

### 5. The 1% Rule of Pricing Validation

In B2B SaaS pricing literature (HubSpot, Price Intelligently, ProfitWell), there's a consistent finding: **you don't know your real price until you've quoted it to 10+ qualified prospects and watched at least 3-5 negotiate on it.** Alizé has quoted zero. The entire 73-pulse debate has produced zero real price reactions from the market.

### Assumption Challenged #4: "Pricing architecture before market contact"

**The entire premise of "finalize pricing before going to market" is a planning fallacy.**

The planning fallacy (Buehler, Griffin, & Ross, 1994; Kahneman & Tversky) describes the tendency to underestimate time, cost, and risk while overestimating predictability. Louis is doing exactly this — treating pricing as a planning problem that can be solved before execution, rather than as a learning problem that requires execution to solve.

**There's no pricing architecture that will survive contact with the market intact. All pricing is provisional until tested.**

## The Real Risk Louis Is Running

Louis isn't avoiding a pricing risk by deferring market contact. He's avoiding market contact and calling it a pricing problem. The actual risks are:

1. **Time risk:** Every week of internal debate is a week without real pricing data. If a competitor goes to market first with any price, they'll learn faster.
2. **Opportunity risk:** The market has signals about what it will pay. Those signals are not being captured.
3. **Confidence risk:** Louis will arrive at his first sales call with a "finalized" price he believes in, but has no evidence for. When the prospect pushes back (and they will), he'll have no basis for negotiation.

**In B2B sales, the first price is always wrong. The question is whether you're wrong with data or wrong without it.**
