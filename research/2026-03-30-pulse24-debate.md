## Debate 1: Louis Solo Delivery — D113 Challenged

### Assumption Challenged
**D113: "Louis delivers pilot 1 solo — this IS the delivery education"**

### Counter-Argument

D113 conflates "learning by doing" with "delivering a client result." These are not the same thing. Learning delivery by delivering on a real client is a high-risk education strategy because:

**1. Louis has no client-facing delivery track record.**
Louis is a web developer and M1 master student. He has never run a discovery call with a PME decision-maker, scoped a business workflow, managed client expectations mid-engagement, or produced a deliverable that a client paid for in this domain. The "learn by doing" model works when failure is cheap. It does not work when failure damages the first reference relationship.

**2. The first client relationship is the most fragile.**
A first pilot client is taking a risk on an unknown brand. If Louis fumbles the discovery call, misqualifies the workflow scope, or delivers something that doesn't work on the first try, the client doesn't say "it's fine, he's learning." They remember a bad experience and never convert. And they certainly don't provide a case study.

**3. D113 and D90 are in direct conflict.**
D90 says "commercial layer gates outreach until closing-capable person exists." But D113 puts Louis — a non-closer — in the most client-facing role of all: delivery. The commercial risk is managed but the delivery risk is hand-waved away.

**4. "Solo delivery" ignores U112 which is still unresolved.**
U112 asks: "Can Louis actually deliver pilot 1 solo? What technical + client-facing skills does he need?" The answer to U112 was never recorded. D113 was decided without resolving U112 — it was decided because the alternatives (find partner first) created circular dependencies.

### The Real Problem
The D113 decision was made reactively, to escape a circular dependency (D48 vs D109). The circular dependency is real, but the solution (Louis solo delivery) doesn't solve it — it defers it. Louis learns delivery on a real client, produces a mediocre result, and then needs to find a partner who can evaluate his mediocre work.

### Recommendation
- **Kill D113 as stated.** Louis should NOT be solo delivery for a paying/reference-seeking client.
- **New model:** Simulate the first delivery before doing it for real. Role-play discovery call with a peer (Gabin, Maëli). Run the technical workflow on a fake dataset. Document what breaks.
- **D113 revised:** Month 1 = simulated delivery rehearsal with peer roleplay, NOT live client delivery.
- **U112 must resolve before Month 1:** Can Louis run a client-facing discovery call? If not, who fills that gap?

### New Unresolved Item
- **U117:** What does Louis's client-facing readiness look like? Can he run a discovery call, manage expectations, and course-correct mid-engagement? Self-assessment needed Week 1.

## Debate 2: CAC Pilot Economics — D110/D111 Challenged

### Assumption Challenged
**D110: First 3 pilots at €1,500-2,000 as CAC investment. D111: Case study rights as commercial term.**

### Counter-Argument

**1. The capital question is unasked.**
D110 assumes €4,500-6,000 in lost pilot revenue is an acceptable marketing cost. But who is paying for this? Louis is an M1 intern. Is he personally funding Alizé? Does he have runway to absorb €6,000 in losses before any revenue? If Alizé has no funding, this plan requires Louis to front €6,000 with zero return for 3+ months. That's not a CAC model — that's personal financial risk.

**2. "Case study rights" has near-zero value to pilot 1-3 clients.**
What exactly is a first pilot client getting?
- A €1,500-2,000 discount (that's what they're getting — the discount)
- "Case study rights" is a fiction at this stage. You don't have a case study to sell them on. You're asking them to fund your marketing in exchange for the privilege of being your test case. The commercial term is undefined and unenforceable.

**3. There's a better model: performance-based first pilot.**
Instead of flat discount for case study rights, structure it as:
- **Free pilot** for the first client if they accept: no case study rights, no testimonial obligation, pure performance test
- **Full price pilot** for the second client with case study rights included
- The first client gets a free pilot with zero strings attached (maximum goodwill)
- The second client pays full price but gets a reference from client #1

Or alternatively:
- **Revenue share:** pilot is free, but Alizé takes 20% of measured time savings for 6 months
- Client pays nothing upfront, Alizé benefits proportionally if the agent actually saves time

**4. The €7,500 unlock logic is backwards.**
The plan says: after 3 reference clients, you can charge €7,500. But the case studies that unlock €7,500 pricing need to be compelling enough to justify that price. If pilots 1-3 are cut-rate deals with desperate clients, the case studies will reflect that desperation. "We paid €1,500 and got a working agent" is not a €7,500 story.

### Recommendation
- **Kill the CAC model as defined.** Replace with: **Pilot 1 = free (pure performance test, zero strings, internal use only as proof)**. Pilot 2 = full price with case study rights. This separates "proving the model works" from "building a sales asset."
- **U118:** What is Louis's actual financial capacity to fund Alizé Month 1-3? Does he have personal runway or is he expecting revenue from Grinto internship?
- **U119:** Define what "case study rights" actually means in practice — usage restrictions, approval timeline, anonymity options, timeline to publication.

### New Unresolved Item
- **U118:** Louis's financial capacity for Alizé Month 1-3. Determines whether CAC model is even viable or whether free-first-pilot is the only option.
- **U119:** Legal definition of "case study rights" — what does the client actually sign?

## Debate 3: Meeting Report Generation as First Workflow — D81 Challenged

### Assumption Challenged
**D81: Meeting report generation and distribution as first workflow beachhead. Rationale: explainable in one sentence, ROI in 30 days, no sensitive data categories.**

### Counter-Argument

**1. "No sensitive data categories" is a liability, not an asset.**
Meeting reports are about as operationally critical as a fancy calendar integration. If the agent fails, the client resends the email manually. There's no urgency, no cost of failure, no reason for the client to care enough to pay €1,500/month after the pilot ends.

D81's rationale ("explainable in one sentence, ROI in 30 days") describes a proof-of-concept demo, not a business-critical workflow. You can demo meeting report generation to any DG in 5 minutes. You cannot demo it in a way that makes them feel the visceral pain of losing it.

**2. Meeting reports don't create case studies that unlock €7,500 pricing.**
The case study from a meeting report pilot reads: "Client saved 15 minutes/week generating meeting reports." That's not a €7,500 story. That's a €200/month SaaS tool story. The pilots that unlock premium pricing need to show operationally critical outcomes: tickets resolved, invoices processed, contracts reviewed, compliance gaps flagged.

**3. The regulated industry wedge (D97/U101) was rejected without resolution.**
D97 says "consider narrowing to regulated industries where Copilot governance gaps are disqualifying." U101 asks "is there a regulated industry wedge where Copilot's gaps are disqualifying enough?" The answer to U101 was never given. Instead D81 picked the safest possible workflow — one with zero governance requirements, zero regulatory sensitivity, zero competitive differentiation from a €20/month SaaS tool.

**4. The first workflow should be chosen for its CASE STUDY VALUE, not its LEARNING VALUE.**
Meeting reports are easy to learn and easy to deploy. They're also easy for any competitor to replicate and hard to charge premium for. The first workflow should be one that:
- Is operationally critical (client feels the loss when it's gone)
- Creates a compelling before/after story with real numbers
- Has natural upsell potential (more workflows, more integrations)
- Cannot be replicated by a €20/month Copilot plugin

**Candidate reconsidered:** Invoice data extraction + expense categorization. Every PME has invoices. It's operationally critical (Finance cares deeply about cash flow). It's measurable (euros saved, hours saved). It's technically achievable in a single integration (connect to email or an accounting tool). And the case study writes itself: "We processed 340 invoices in month 1, saving the finance team 12 hours."

### Recommendation
- **U120:** Re-evaluate D81 against case study value criteria, not learning curve criteria. What workflow produces the most compelling proof point for a €7,500 pricing conversation?
- **U121:** Could regulated industries (legal, accounting, healthcare admin) with governance requirements be the wedge that makes Alizé's first workflow defensible against Copilot?
- Month 1: Test two workflow candidates with warm contacts before committing to one. Get real feedback on which feels more operationally critical.

### New Unresolved Item
- **U120:** Is meeting report generation the right first workflow, or should it be something operationally critical with better case study value?
- **U121:** Is there a regulated industry wedge where governance requirements are disqualifying for Copilot — making Alizé's governance story the primary differentiator from day 1?

## Summary of What Changed

1. **Delivery:** D113 (Louis solo delivery) is too risky for a reference relationship. Month 1 should be simulated rehearsal, not live client delivery.
2. **Pricing:** The CAC model (€1,500 pilots) assumes capital Louis may not have and creates case studies of desperation, not proof points. Free first pilot separates proof of concept from sales asset building.
3. **First workflow:** Meeting report generation is demo-friendly but not operationally critical enough to justify €7,500/month or produce a compelling case study. A more operationally critical workflow (invoice processing, compliance checking) would serve better as the beachhead.
