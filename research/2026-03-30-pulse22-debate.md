## DEBATE 1: The Web Agency Conflict — Is Maëli/Gabin's Agency a Valid Internal Pilot Target?
**Challenged Decision:** D100 — "Internal pilot target = web agency (Maëli/Gabin web agency for Month 1 delivery rehearsal)"

### The Assumptions Being Challenged
1. The web agency is a "clean" internal target with no conflict
2. A 2-3 person web agency is a valid proxy for Alizé's 50-200 employee PME ICP

### Arguments Against D100

**Conflict of Interest — Alizé's ICP overlaps with the web agency's business.**
D77 frames Alizé's target as "back-office admin AI for service firms." The web agency's own clients are service firms — they build websites for the exact companies Alizé wants to pitch. When a web agency client asks Maëli or Gabin "can you also automate our client onboarding workflow?" or "can you build an AI agent for our support queue?", the web agency becomes a direct competitor to Alizé's managed service offering. Using the web agency as the internal delivery rehearsal target means:
- Alizé trains the web agency on its own playbook
- The web agency then has the operational knowledge to compete with Alizé on agent deployments
- The playbook built during the rehearsal is partially owned by a future competitive threat

**Scale mismatch invalidates the rehearsal.**
A 2-3 person web agency has no organizational complexity. Decision-makers are the founders. Workflows are ad-hoc. Integration points are minimal. Alizé's ICP is 50-200 person companies with real organizational structure — where an agent deployment touches multiple employees, requires governance buy-in from non-technical stakeholders, and integrates with real business tools (Sage, Cegid, Pennylane, etc.).

Running a delivery rehearsal against a 2-person agency teaches Louis almost nothing about deploying to a 100-person accounting firm. The deployment complexity gap is not incremental — it is categorical.

**D100 creates a knowledge asymmetry problem.**
The delivery rehearsal's value is in building a playbook (D88). If the rehearsal is done with the web agency, the playbook contains web-agency-specific assumptions: simple workflows, founder-level approval, minimal compliance requirements. When Louis applies that playbook to a 100-person target, the assumptions won't transfer. He'll have to unlearn behaviors learned in the rehearsal.

**U95 is not merely an "unresolved item" — it is a blocking constraint.**
The web agency-Alizé conflict (U95) should have been resolved BEFORE D100 was adopted. The sequence was backwards: D100 was decided, then U95 remained "unresolved." Any decision that creates a competitive transfer of Alizé's core asset (the delivery playbook) to a co-founder's competing business needs to be examined before it is ratified.

### Arguments For D100 (Steel Man)

The web agency has the advantage of zero external pressure — Louis can push without worrying about a client relationship. It's the fastest path to a live deployment that produces measurable outcomes. And if the web agency is not a "real" Alizé client, there's no case study value anyway, so the conflict may be theoretical.

### Verdict

**D100 is premature and creates an unexamined conflict.** Before the Month 1 delivery rehearsal proceeds with the web agency:
1. U95 must be resolved: does the web agency see itself as competing with Alizé for client AI deployments? If yes, the conflict is real and disqualifying.
2. If the web agency is used, the playbook must be explicitly scoped to exclude any knowledge that could be used to compete with Alizé.
3. A better internal target: Louis himself as the "pilot" — running the meeting-report workflow on his own work, or deploying to Grinto (his internship company) with permission.

## DEBATE 2: Pricing Viability — Can €2,000-2,500/Month Hold Without a Senior Commercial Closer?
**Challenged Decisions:** D84, D90 — Louis cannot close, but someone must. Pricing only works if conversion is possible.

### The Assumption Being Challenged
That the pricing model (€7,500 pilot, €2,000-2,500/month retainer) is viable in principle, and the only problem is finding a commercial co-founder to execute it. This framing ignores that the price point itself may be the conversion barrier — not just the lack of a closing person.

### Arguments Against Current Pricing

**At €2,000/month, Alizé is selling a service that buyers cannot easily evaluate.**
Managed service at €2,000/month for a single workflow is expensive relative to alternatives. Agentova is €49-99/agent/month. ChatGPT Teams is €22/user. A French PME with 50 employees spending €22/user for ChatGPT Teams costs €1,100/month for the entire company. Alizé at €2,000/month for one workflow is nearly 2x that — for one department's automation. The value proposition requires a DG buyer to believe that ONE workflow automation is worth more than their entire company's AI tooling budget. That's a very hard sell.

**The "governance and French hosting" differentiator does not justify a 20x premium over Agentova.**
Agentova's market existence proves French SMBs will pay €49-99/month for AI agents. Alizé at €2,000/month is asking for a 20-40x premium. The question the pricing model has never answered: what does Alizé deliver that justifies 20x more than a self-service tool? "We manage it for you" is a service claim, not a value claim. Managed service is a delivery model, not a reason to pay.

**Louis's credibility gap compounds the pricing problem.**
A 21-year-old M1 student with no professional track record is not someone a DG at a 100-person company pays €7,500 to. The price point assumes the seller has credibility. Louis cannot manufacture credibility through preparation — it comes from demonstrated professional experience, which he doesn't have yet. The pricing model requires a credible seller, but no credible seller has been secured.

**The conversion mechanism (D28: €4,500 pilot + month 1 €1,200 with 50% refund) doesn't reduce the risk at €7,500.**
50% of €7,500 is €3,750 at risk. For a 100-person company spending €1,500-2,000/month on operational tools, €3,750 is a meaningful commitment. The refund guarantee doesn't make the decision easier — it makes Alizé look uncertain about its own value. "We're so confident we'll refund half if you don't like it" reads as a vendor admitting the product might fail.

### Arguments For Current Pricing (Steel Man)

The pricing reflects actual delivery costs: 10-15 days of consultant time at €400-450/day = €4,000-6,750 in delivery costs alone. At €2,000/month, Alizé is not extracting margin — it is covering costs. The market rate for French ESN consulting is €600-900/day. Alizé at €400-450/day to a delivery partner is already discounted. The price is honest about costs, even if it feels expensive to buyers.

### Verdict

**The pricing model has a conversion problem, not just a closing-person problem.** D84 and D90 identified the symptom (Louis can't close) but not the underlying issue (the price point itself may be unsellable without credibility + differentiation that doesn't yet exist). Three concrete alternatives:
1. **Lower the pilot to €1,500-2,000** for first 2-3 clients in exchange for case study rights + testimonial video. Accept losses on pilot clients as CAC.
2. **Package the first workflow as a fixed-scope project** at €3,000-5,000 delivered, not €7,500. Frame it as "your first AI workflow, built and running."
3. **Don't charge for the pilot** — deliver one workflow free, then ask for a 6-month commitment. The free workflow is the sales tool.

## DEBATE 3: Delivery Credibility — What Minimum Signals Are Needed Before a DG Signs?
**Challenged Decisions:** D63, D89 — delivery partner needed; D88 — playbook as moat

### The Assumption Being Challenged
That a delivery partner from Malt (freelance consultant marketplace) can provide the delivery credibility Alizé needs. This conflates "has consulting experience" with "can credibly design and deploy an AI agent workflow for a 100-person company."

### Arguments Against Malt-Found Delivery Partner

**A €400/day freelance consultant has no brand equity with the DG buyer.**
A DG at a 100-person French company receives cold outreach from consultants weekly. The Malt marketplace is where companies find IT contractors, not where a DG goes to find strategic AI deployment partners. A consultant found on Malt, at €400/day, is perceived as a contractor — not as an Alizé representative. When that consultant shows up to a client site or joins a discovery call, the client sees a freelancer, not Alizé.

**The delivery partner model separates accountability from expertise.**
D89 frames the delivery partner as co-author of the playbook. But a contractor has no incentive to build Alizé's intellectual property — they have an incentive to complete their billable work and move on. The playbook-as-moat (D88) requires someone with a long-term stake in Alizé's success to write it. A €400/day contractor is structurally incapable of being that person.

**Louis cannot supervise delivery quality he cannot perform himself.**
D63 requires Louis to find a delivery partner who can execute at quality. But Louis has no delivery experience — he cannot evaluate whether a workflow has been designed well, whether the agent's outputs are correct, or whether the client's team has been properly trained. Louis is trusting a contractor's quality on a discipline he doesn't know himself. This is not a governance structure — it is a blind spot with a line item.

### Minimum Credibility Stack Required

Before a DG signs a €7,500 pilot, Alizé needs:
1. **A named reference from a similar-size company** — not "here's what we did for ourselves" but "here's what we did for Acme SARL, 80 employees, in the same industry." This requires a third-party client.
2. **A face behind the service** — Louis as a 21-year-old with no track record is not credible for €7,500. The delivery partner must have demonstrated PME consulting experience (not freelance IT), with verifiable references from companies of similar size.
3. **A deliverable the DG can evaluate immediately** — not "here's what the agent will do in 4 weeks" but "here's what it looks like." A live demonstration of a working agent on a real workflow (even a simple one) changes the conversation from "will this work?" to "how do we get started?"

### Verdict

**D63/D89 solve the wrong problem.** Hiring a delivery partner adds a warm body but doesn't add credibility. The credibility problem requires:
- A senior commercial figure who can represent Alizé in the room (not Louis alone)
- A third-party reference client (or an internal rehearsal with outcomes convincing enough to show a DG)
- A live working agent demonstration — even a simple one

Without these, the delivery partner is an execution resource, not a credibility resource. Alizé will still struggle to close at the current price point regardless of who delivers.
