# Competitive Response Challenge — 2026-03-31

## Assumption Challenged

**That Alizé's competitive positioning against Microsoft/Copilot is adequate and that competitive framing is a priority worth spending messaging real estate on.**

---

## Arguments For: Why the Existing Competitive Response Might Work

### 1. The "Copilot answers questions, Alizé runs workflows" framing is pithy and memorable
It is a clean distinction. Questions vs. actions. Passive vs. active. A non-technical buyer can grasp it instantly. If a prospect is already bought into Microsoft and has Copilot, this tells them there's a next step without requiring them to abandon what they've already invested in.

### 2. It positions Alizé as complementary, not adversarial
D96 said "never name Microsoft externally" — KILLED. But the instinct was right: naming a dominant competitor can lend them legitimacy. The D139 framing sidesteps this by positioning Alizé as the *next layer*, not a replacement. Buyers who like Microsoft but want more can self-select into Alizé.

### 3. It is directionally accurate
Copilot does primarily answer questions. Alizé does run workflows. The framing is not *wrong* — it is *under-specified*. The problem is not accuracy, it's sufficiency.

### 4. If Copilot adoption among French PME is actually low (U98), the framing is moot anyway
If French PME haven't widely adopted Copilot, then "when Copilot isn't enough" is preemptive framing for a problem that doesn't exist yet at the target segment. The framing only matters if the prospect is already a Copilot user — and there is zero evidence they are.

---

## Arguments Against: Why It Likely Fails

### Problem 1: The Framing Tells Buyers to Buy Microsoft First

"Copilot answers questions. Alizé runs workflows" is a **sequential pitch**, not a differentiation. Read it from the buyer's perspective:

> "So I should buy Microsoft 365, get Copilot, and then if I need more, call Alizé?"

This is a recipe for Alizé being a *secondary purchase* at best, and an upsell on top of an existing Microsoft contract at worst. It cedes the primary vendor relationship to Microsoft and positions Alizé as a premium add-on. In a budget-constrained French PME, "add-on" means "first to cut when IT spending tightens."

This is the opposite of a competitive response. It is a channel partnership offer to Microsoft.

### Problem 2: The Framing Has No Legs Without Proof

For this framing to work, Alizé must demonstrate, in every sales conversation:

- That Copilot genuinely *cannot* run the proposed workflows
- That Alizé *can* run those workflows today
- That the workflow execution is reliable, measurable, and differentiated

Alizé has zero pilots, zero case studies, and zero documented conversion evidence. The framing is a claim without proof. A claim without proof is marketing. Marketing without evidence is noise.

### Problem 3: The D97 "Governance Disqualifies Copilot" Was Killed — But No Replacement Mechanism Was Found

D97 argued that governance gaps in regulated industries would disqualify Copilot. This was contradicted by D127 (expert-comptables killed — governance gap rationale didn't hold). The D97 hypothesis was:

> "Copilot has governance gaps → regulated industries can't use it → Alizé wins by default"

This was never validated. After 54 pulses, the specific mechanism by which Alizé *wins* in any segment vs. Copilot remains **unstated and unresearched**. The debate log shows vigorous discussion of positioning options but no primary research answering:

- What % of proposed Alizé use cases Copilot can execute today? (U100 — unresolved)
- What is the actual French PME Copilot adoption rate? (U98 — unresolved after 54 pulses)
- What is the specific governance gap that disqualifies Copilot for any target segment? (not answered)

Without these answers, the entire competitive response is built on assumptions that have been repeatedly identified as unvalidated and never resolved.

### Problem 4: "Not Positioning Against Microsoft" May Be the Smarter Move

There is a coherent world where Alizé does not mention Microsoft at all. In this world:

- Alizé leads with vertical outcomes: "AI agents for French accounting firms that close the monthly close 3 days faster"
- Alizé never mentions Copilot because the prospect may not have Copilot (U98: unknown adoption rate)
- Alizé competes on *results*, not on feature comparison against a $12/user/month product that the prospect may not even be using
- The competitive frame becomes: "Alizé vs. doing it manually" not "Alizé vs. Microsoft"

This avoids the sequential pitch problem entirely. Why tell a prospect who has never bought Copilot that Alizé is the step after Copilot?

### Problem 5: Competitive Positioning Is Premature Without Market Evidence

This is the core challenge:

**Alizé has:**
- Zero pilots
- Zero case studies
- Zero documented conversion evidence
- No resolved answers to U98 or U100

**Competitive positioning requires:**
- A clear understanding of what the competitor actually does (U100)
- Evidence that the competitor fails at something specific (unvalidated)
- Proof that Alizé succeeds where they fail (zero case studies)

Spending energy on competitive messaging when the fundamental questions — *what does Alizé do that works, and for whom?* — are unanswered is putting the cart before the horse.

## Recommendation

### Step 0: Answer U98 and U100 Before Spending Another Pulse on Competitive Positioning

If U98 (PME Copilot adoption) is low, the entire competitive frame against Microsoft is irrelevant for the target segment. If U100 shows Copilot can execute 80% of proposed Alizé use cases today, the "Alizé runs workflows" claim requires a far more specific articulation of *which workflows and why*.

**Stop debating positioning. Go find out.**

### Step 1: If the Competitive Frame Stays, Make It a Outcome Frame, Not a Feature Comparison

"Copilot answers questions, Alizé runs workflows" is a feature comparison. Feature comparisons require you to be in the same room as the evaluation.

If Alizé leads with outcomes — specific, quantified, vertical outcomes — the competitive frame becomes irrelevant. The question isn't "Alizé vs. Copilot" it's "close the monthly accounting close 3 days faster vs. not."

### Step 2: The "Not Microsoft" Option Deserves Serious Consideration

The "never name Microsoft externally" instinct (D96 — killed) was right in principle even if the execution was wrong. Consider a world where:

- Alizé never mentions Microsoft
- Alizé competes against "doing it manually" and "hiring more staff"
- Alizé wins on vertical depth, not on feature parity claims
- The competitive intelligence about Microsoft is used *internally* to inform product, not *externally* for messaging

This is a cleaner, more honest positioning. It avoids the sequential pitch problem. It doesn't require proof that Copilot is insufficient (which doesn't exist). It focuses on what Alizé *is*, not what it *isn't*.

### Step 3: If D97's Mechanism Was Killed, Find a New One or Stop Trying to Win on Governance

The D97 hypothesis (governance gaps disqualify Copilot) was contradicted. No replacement mechanism has been identified. Rather than cycling through positioning variations on the same broken logic, the team should either:

- Do primary research to find a *real* mechanism (e.g., data residency requirements that actually block Copilot for specific segments)
- Accept that governance is a feature, not a differentiator, and lead with something else

### Step 4: Accept That Competitive Positioning Is Secondary to Having Something to Sell

With zero pilots, zero case studies, and zero conversion evidence, the most urgent competitive question is not "how do we position against Microsoft?" It's "do we have a product that anyone would buy, and can we prove it?"

Competitive positioning is a luxury of companies with evidence. Alizé is not there yet.

---

## Final Verdict

**The assumption that Alizé's competitive response is adequate is not supported by the evidence.**

The current framing ("Copilot answers questions, Alizé runs workflows") is:
- Directionally accurate but insufficiently differentiating
- A sequential pitch that cedes primary vendor status to Microsoft
- Premised on unresolved questions (U98, U100) about whether the competitive threat even exists at the target segment
- Built on a governance mechanism (D97) that was killed and never replaced

**The smarter move is probably to stop positioning against Microsoft entirely** and focus on vertical, outcome-led positioning that answers: "what does Alizé do that works, for whom, and can we prove it?"

Until that question is answered, competitive positioning is a distraction.
