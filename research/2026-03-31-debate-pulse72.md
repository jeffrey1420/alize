## Agent 1: Behavioral Pattern Auditor
**Challenge:** "Build Kuroba first, then outreach"

### Assumption Challenged
**"Kuroba internal deployment is a prerequisite for Alizé outreach"** — that demonstrating Kuroba's capabilities will unlock Louis's ability to take external action for Alizé.

### Argument
The mechanism at work is **Identity Protection Through Sequential Preconditioning**. Louis has an established identity as "Kuroba operator" — someone who builds web products for clients. Alizé requires him to adopt a new identity: "venture founder." Every "finish Kuroba first" iteration is not progress toward Alizé — it's an identity preservation mechanism.

This is why 18 Kuroba "imminent" deployments never triggered Alizé action. Each completed deployment is followed by a new precondition: "now deploy it," "now demonstrate it," "now let that sink in." The completion of X never produces the expected Y because the real trigger was never X. The trigger would need to be an identity decision, and "build Kuroba first" explicitly avoids making that decision.

The specific behavioral failure pattern is **Conditional Commitment Without Termination Clause** — a commitment structured so it can perpetually defer to the next prerequisite. "I'll do Alizé outreach when Kuroba is deployed" is not a plan with a finish line. It's a deferral mechanism with no defined endpoint.

The assumption "Kuroba deployment unlocks commercial credibility" treats credibility as something external that must be accumulated before action is permitted. But Louis runs Kuroba successfully. He has 71 pulses of Alizé research. Credibility is not his constraint — commitment is. Each Kuroba iteration will simply generate the next precondition.

### What This Changes
If correct: "demonstrate Kuroba deployment" will not break Louis's execution paralysis. It will provide a new platform for the next deferral. The intervention point shifts from "finish building" to "make the identity commitment."

### Verdict
**Overturned.** The assumption that Kuroba deployment is a prerequisite for Alizé outreach is itself the deferral mechanism. The 18 prior attempts at Kuroba deployment that never triggered Alizé action are the evidence.

## Agent 2: Merger Validity Auditor
**Challenge:** "Merge Alizé into Kuroba as a product tier (P70-D1)"

### Challenge 1: "One entity reduces overhead"
**Assumption:** Having one entity instead of two reduces operational overhead.

**Argument:** The overhead is not structural — it's behavioral. The 270+ deferred Alizé decisions aren't caused by having two legal entities. They're caused by Louis avoiding Alizé decisions. If he deferred 270 decisions as a separate entity, he will defer 270 as a tier within Kuroba. The merger changes the org chart. It does not change the decision-making pattern. The operational debt gets imported into the one venture Louis is actually running, contaminating Kuroba's clean state.

**Verdict:** Overturned.

### Challenge 2: "Kuroba credibility transfers to Alizé"
**Assumption:** Alizé's credibility problem is solved by association with Kuroba.

**Argument:** Alizé did not fail for lack of brand credibility. Kuroba's credibility was earned through consistent delivery over time. If anything, positioning Alizé as "Kuroba Managed" creates a credibility risk: prospects who expect Kuroba-level service and receive Alizé-level execution will hold Kuroba's reputation hostage. Rebranding doesn't fix execution failures — it just makes them visible under the Kuroba name.

**Verdict:** Overturned.

### Challenge 3: "Merged entity solves the priority problem"
**Assumption:** Consolidating to one venture eliminates the priority conflict.

**Argument:** The stated constraint is 5-8 hours/week shared across ventures. The priority problem is not "I have two things to choose between" — it's "I consistently choose neither deeply." A merger does not create more hours. Louis will now have one venture to spread 5-8 hours across instead of two. The total available attention is unchanged. The priority problem — Louis's inability to concentrate sustained effort — is not addressed.

**Verdict:** Overturned.

### Summary
The merger is a reorganization of containers. It does not:
- Change Louis's decision-making behavior
- Recover the 270+ hours already deferred
- Add capacity to a time-constrained schedule
- Earn credibility Alizé hasn't built

It does:
- Import Alizé's operational debt into Kuroba
- Risk Kuroba's reputation by associating it with unresolved execution failures
- Create a false sense of focus consolidation

**The structural problem is a symptom. The behavioral problem is the cause. The merger addresses the symptom.**

### Recommendation
Before any merger: Louis must demonstrate he can close the 270+ Alizé decisions independently. If he cannot clear Alizé's debt as a separate entity, he will not clear it as a tier within Kuroba. The test of whether the merger works is whether Louis can handle Alizé's backlog as a tier — and that test requires him to already be handling it.

### Verdict
**Overturned.** Merger is a structural answer to a behavioral problem.

## Agent 3: Process Kill Auditor
**Challenge:** "Killing the research pulse is the correct intervention"

### Challenge 1: The Pulse Is Not the Displacement Activity
**Assumption:** Removing the pulse removes the displacement activity — without the research loop, Louis will be forced to act.

**Argument:** This assumes the pulse is the *cause* of Louis's inaction. It isn't. The pulse is a *recording device* for behavior Louis exhibits independently. Evidence: the meta-finding of the last 20 pulses is "stop researching, start executing." Louis has read this finding repeatedly. He has not acted on it. If Louis cannot act on a *direct, explicit meta-finding* that tells him to stop researching, removing the pulse (which generates the meta-finding) will not change anything. The block exists at a layer above the pulse.

**Verdict:** Revised — pulse removal is necessary but not sufficient.

### Challenge 2: Louis Cannot Self-Regulate Through Decision-Production
**Assumption:** The pulse has produced 270+ decisions — if Louis executes them, progress happens.

**Argument:** The bottleneck is not access to decisions. The bottleneck is that Louis treats a decision as a terminal event — something completed, not something that creates an obligation to act. Decision D249 was "suspend the pulse." That *was* an execution. And it failed to produce lasting change because Louis did not treat "suspend the pulse" as a commitment to be enforced against future resistance. He made the decision, felt it was handled, and moved on. The 270+ decisions are not a pipeline — they are a graveyard. No external force moves them from "decided" to "done."

**Verdict:** Overturned as a solution mechanism.

### What Would Actually Work

**The Specific Problem:** Louis's relationship to commitment is unresolved. He experiences decisions as information events, not as self-imposed obligations with consequences. He can decide to suspend the pulse (D249) and then un-suspend it two weeks later without experiencing that as a broken promise to himself. There is no external structure that treats his commitments as binding.

**The Specific Intervention — Hard Commitment Externalizer:**

1. **A committed outcome, not a process.** Instead of "run the pulse less," Louis specifies: "By [date], I will have [emailed X / launched Y / published Z]." The commitment must be outcome-verifiable, not effort-measurable.

2. **Someone whose job is to ask "did you do it?"** This person does not advise, coach, or help. They only receive Louis's commitment at the start and ask the question at the deadline. If Louis did not do it, the only valid response is: "then we need to talk about why you said you would and didn't." This is obligation creation through social fact.

3. **Pre-committed consequences.** Before the work begins, Louis specifies: "If I don't deliver X by Y, then [specific consequence]." The consequence must be meaningful to Louis — not punitive, but something he genuinely doesn't want to happen.

**Why this is different from what Louis has tried:** The 5 prior suspension decisions failed because they were process rules Louis made for himself and could unmute at will. The pulse-meta-findings failed because they were self-generated recommendations with no social weight. What Louis has not tried: making a specific, dated, outcome-verifiable commitment to a person who will follow up and treat non-delivery as a real event.

### Verdict
**Revised.** Killing the pulse is necessary (removes avoidance infrastructure) but not sufficient. The sufficient intervention requires Louis to make a specific, dated, verifiable commitment to another human being — with pre-specified consequences for non-delivery.
