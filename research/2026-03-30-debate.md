## Challenge 2: The "Binary Choice" Is the Wrong Frame — The Real Question Is Legal Capacity

The research describes U91's 91-day paralysis as a **commitment failure**. Louis has not decided whether Alizé is a startup or a side-project. The binary choice is framed as the forcing function that unlocks all other decisions.

**But what if the real question is not "what is Alizé?" but "can Louis legally be Alizé at all?"**

The research makes passing reference to U94 ("Grinto exit timeline") and U116 ("Grinto ring-fencing feasibility") as unresolved. These are dismissed as "legal review Week 1" items. They have been unresolved for 44+ pulses. Here is what those items actually represent:

### The Grinto Contract Problem

Grinto is Louis's current employer. As an intern or employee, Louis likely signed:

- **IP assignment clause:** Any intellectual property created during employment may belong to Grinto — including Alizé's workflows, agent architectures, or positioning research
- **Non-compete or exclusivity clause:** Depending on jurisdiction and contract terms, Louis may be restricted from operating a competing business during his employment
- **Side venture restrictions:** Many French employment contracts require disclosure and approval for external business activities

**If Louis's Grinto contract contains IP assignment or exclusivity provisions, Louis cannot legally:**
- Sign client agreements for Alizé (the work product may belong to Grinto)
- Own alize.studio in his personal capacity for a competing business
- Sign a shareholder agreement with a commercial co-founder (Alizé equity may encumber Grinto IP)

### The Auto-Entrepreneur Problem

If Louis is already operating as an auto-entrepreneur (which the research mentions in the context of delivery partner contracting), he faces different constraints:

- **Activity restrictions:** Auto-entrepreneur status is capped by activity type. If "AI agent services for regulated SMEs" falls outside his registered activity codes, he cannot legally invoice for it
- **Exclusivity thresholds:** Auto-entrepreneur status has revenue ceilings. Additional business activities may trigger structural changes
- **Client concentration rules:** Some auto-entrepreneur regimes restrict serving certain client types

**The binary choice framing ("startup vs. side-project") is a category error. Louis doesn't need to choose between two types of venture. He needs to know whether he can operate any venture at all under his current legal status.**

### Agent 3: Kuroba Proof-Point Credibility Challenge

**Assumption challenged:** D194/D195 — that Kuroba (Louis's co-founded web agency) serves as the ideal first internal proof point and strongest social proof because "we built it for ourselves first."

**Core argument:**

D187 correctly identified that Louis co-founding a web agency creates competitive conflict. D194/D195 correctly responded that Kuroba is the ideal first deployment target. Both are right. The contradiction is the assumption underneath both: that Maëli and Gabin will cooperate with Louis-as-Alizé-founder in sharing their internal workflows.

**Three specific challenges:**

1. **Kuroba's cooperation is not guaranteed.** Louis is a co-founder of Kuroba. Maëli and Gabin are co-founders. If Louis asks them to share their internal workflows (client communication, project management, billing processes) for Alizé's first proof point, he's asking them to: (a) give Alizé access to their business data, (b) participate in a demo that may eventually be shown to Kuroba's competitors, (c) potentially expose what's working and not working in their own operations. Alizé and Kuroba are not the same company. Maëli and Gabin have no obligation to help Louis build a competing venture's proof point.

2. **The "we built it for ourselves" social proof works only if the audience cares.** "We built it for ourselves" is a strong proof point when the builder and the buyer are similar in size, complexity, and problem type. Kuroba is a 2-3 person web agency. Alizé's ICP is a 20-50 person service firm. A 50-person service firm CFO watching a demo of a 2-person agency's workflow automation is not going to think "that could work for us." The scale gap is too large.

3. **D220's "service firm client communication automation" scope faces a proof problem.** If Kuroba's workflows are web agency project management and client comms, and Alizé's first target is "service firms" (consulting, reporting, administrative), then Kuroba's workflow demo does NOT show what Alizé claims to do. The demo must match the claim. A web agency comms automation demo does not prove an administrative workflow automation capability for consulting firms.

**The hidden assumption:** That "co-founded Kuroba" is Louis's strongest credential for Alizé. But "co-founded a 2-person web agency" is a weak credential for selling AI agents to 50-person service firms. It demonstrates technical competence, not domain expertise or operational scale credibility.

**Recommendation:** Kuroba is a legitimate internal learning target, not a sales proof point. Build the internal deployment, learn from it, but do not treat it as social proof for external prospects until the prospect's company looks like Kuroba (2-3 people, web/tech context). For the 50-person service firm ICP, find a different proof mechanism — ideally a pilot with an actual similar-sized company.

**Challenge to existing assumption:** D195 ("we built it for ourselves" is the strongest available social proof) treats proximity as equivalent to credibility. A 2-person web agency's internal automation is not comparable to a 50-person service firm's operational complexity.
