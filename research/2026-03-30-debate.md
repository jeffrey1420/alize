## Agent 1: MCP Defensibility Challenge

**Assumption challenged:** MCP (Model Context Protocol) integration capability is a defensible technical moat for Alizé.

### Key Findings

**1. MCP is an open standard — not a moat.**
MCP was published by Anthropic in November 2024. SDKs exist in Python and TypeScript. Any competitor can implement MCP servers for Slack, Gmail, HubSpot within days. The "7,000+ MCP servers available" cited in architecture docs is the entire ecosystem's inventory — citing it as Alizé's differentiator is like a telecom citing internet availability.

**2. Custom MCP servers per client = commodity dev work.**
"We wrapped the Pennylane API in MCP" is not intellectual property. Any competent dev shop replicates this in weeks. The only compounding question is whether accumulated client configurations create network effects — they don't, not unless there's proprietary business logic inside those servers.

**3. French hosting ≠ EU AI Act compliance.**
GDPR applies EU-wide. OVH's Dutch zone is equally GDPR-compliant. The EU AI Act mandates EU residency, not French hosting specifically. SecNumCloud (ANSSI) certification matters for some public sector clients, but that's one specific certification — not a universal requirement.

**4. What might actually be defensible:**
- Multi-tenant MCP security implementation (battle-tested, hardened) — execution advantage, not structural moat
- Accumulated client configuration templates and onboarding playbooks
- Integration expertise embedded in the team

**Replication timeline for a well-funded competitor:** 2 weeks (demo) → 2 months (production parity) → 4-6 months (full library parity).

### Verdict
**Partially defensible — but not because of MCP.**

The MCP protocol and concept are commodity. What might be defensible is the multi-tenant security implementation (if actually built and tested), accumulated client configuration templates, and the team's integration expertise. These are execution advantages, not structural moats. A well-funded competitor closes the gap in a single product quarter.

### Recommendations
1. Stop treating MCP as a marketing or differentiation claim — demote it to pure infrastructure detail
2. Invest in the multi-tenant security implementation as the real MCP moat, and document it specifically for trust-building with IT/procurement buyers

## Agent 2: Demo-Led Sales Motion Challenge

**Assumption challenged:** D182's "demo-led, prototype-first, zero-pitch" is the correct sales motion for Alizé's price point and audience.

### Key Findings

**1. Demos create interest, not commitment.**
PME DGs and COOs in 2026 have been burned by AI demos that worked in the sandbox and failed in production. A demo proves you can build something — not that it survives contact with their messy CRM, their real team, their actual workflow. The demo becomes a conversation starter, not a closing event. Prospects say "impressive, let me think about it" — and thinking about it is where deals go to die.

**2. Low-friction sales increase post-demo internal friction.**
"Zero-pitch, prototype-first" borrows from consumer SaaS ($20/month individual purchase). Alizé sells at €7,500/month to organizations where the DG needs to defend the purchase upward to associates, boards, or partners. Low-friction acquisition removes sales friction but *increases* post-demo consensus-building friction. The DG now has to sell Alizé internally without sales support.

**3. D183 (free pilots) repeats the D176/D177 mistake.**
Free pilots attract tire-kickers and budget-freezers — companies with no real urgency, no real budget, no real intention to pay. When something is free, the buyer never feels ownership. They run a half-hearted test, and when the pilot ends, say "we need more time to evaluate." This is exactly why D176/D177 were killed.

**4. What actually triggers a signed €7,500 pilot:**
- Trusted peer recommendation from someone in a similar role at a similar company
- Clear, specific ROI calculation tied to a metric the DG already tracks
- Executive relationship and trust built over time

Alizé currently lacks all three. The demo substitutes for the trust and ROI story it doesn't yet have.

### Verdict
**Pitch-led required** — not instead of a demo, but *before* one.

The demo should be earned after initial qualification, trust-building, and ROI framing. D182 assumes the product sells itself. At €7,500/month to a skeptical DG, the product is 30% of the sale. Relationship and proof of business impact are the other 70%.

### Recommendations
1. **Qualify before demo**: Add a 15-minute discovery call focused on the DG's specific operational pain, current tools, and tracked KPIs. Customize the demo narrative *before* showing the prototype.
2. **Replace "free pilot" with "proof-of-value at cost"**: Charge €500-1,000 for a 2-week scoped pilot with defined success metrics and a 30-day follow-up meeting. Creates accountability on both sides, weeds out tire-kickers, gives Alizé actual data to show the next prospect.

## Agent 3: Pricing Anchor Challenge

**Assumption challenged:** Alizé's ROI framing and pricing anchor are correctly calibrated against both the "employee alternative" and the "Copilot additive" comparison.

### Key Findings

**1. The employee ROI comparison is internally incoherent.**
Alizé's pricing (€1,200-2,500/month) sits in the *same price band* as the employee alternative it claims to beat (€1,500-2,500/month for part-time). The ROI case is not "Alizé saves you money vs. an employee" — it's "Alizé costs roughly the same as an employee, trust our capability claims." The buyer has no reason to switch from "I'll just hire someone" when the cost is equivalent and the human feels more controllable.

**2. The employee comparison creates a commodity trap.**
Once buyers frame Alizé as an employee substitute, any price increase looks unreasonable. Alizé can't escape the commodity comparison. This wasn't the intended effect — the employee framing was meant to signal affordability — but that's not how buyers process it.

**3. The "additive to Copilot" framing creates the wrong mental accounting.**
A company already spending €3,200/month on Copilot 365 is in "software subscription" mental accounting. Adding €1,500-2,500/month feels like a natural upgrade to the same budget category — *but only if the framing is "more AI capability" not "replacing a human."* The employee framing takes them out of software subscription mode and into headcount mode, where Alizé suddenly looks expensive.

**4. Frustrated Copilot users ask the wrong question.**
D140 says "buyer frustrated with Copilot" is a buying signal. But frustrated Copilot users typically ask "how do I get more out of Copilot?" not "where can I get a completely different AI system?" Alizé's current framing doesn't address the actual question the frustrated Copilot buyer is asking.

### Verdict
**Current pricing framing needs revision** — specifically, the employee ROI anchor is working against the sale.

It's not wrong tactically; the price band makes it logically incoherent as a value anchor. The framing creates a commodity trap that makes Alizé look expensive relative to its supposed alternative.

### Recommendations
1. **Kill or reanchor the employee comparison.** If used at all, it should establish a minimum value floor — not the ceiling of Alizé's positioning. The framing should be "10x the output of a €2,500/month employee, not the same output at the same price."
2. **Price the pilot to escape employee mental accounting.** Frame the pilot as "2 weeks of Alizé proving what 6 months of an employee would take to learn" — velocity and compounding knowledge, not headcount substitution.
