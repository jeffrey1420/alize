## Agent 1: Delivery Model Strategist

### Assumptions Challenged

**A1: "Implementation contractor by Month 1-2" solves the delivery gap**
The assumption treats the contractor as a drop-in solution. It is not. A contractor brought in at Month 1-2 arrives without Alizé methodology context, without established client relationships, and without incentives aligned to long-term quality. They are a variable in the most critical early delivery scenario.

**A2: "Louis can be client-facing AND oversee platform development"**
This dual role assumption has no buffer. The moment the first pilot client has a problem at 9pm on a Wednesday, Louis must choose between platform firefighting and client crisis response. He cannot do both at quality.

**A3: "Informal delivery is fine until Month 3"**
Informal structures survive until they don't. The first pilot is the reference case. If delivery is scrappy and undocumented, Alizé has no playbook for the second client and no defensible case study for the third.

### The Case Against

**Client relationship fragmentation:** Louis starts the diagnostic conversation. A contractor executes the pilot. The client notices the handoff. Trust erodes. For a service business where the product IS the relationship, this is fatal.

**Contractor dependency without knowledge transfer:** The contractor learns Alizé's delivery methodology through trial and error. When the contract ends, that knowledge leaves. There is no SOP, no runbook, no institutional memory.

**Louis burnout pathway:** Month 1-2 requires: recruiting and onboarding a contractor, managing 2 dev priorities, running client discovery, and delivering the first pilot. The contractor addition does not reduce Louis's load — it adds management overhead.

**No quality floor:** Without documented processes, "done" is whatever the contractor decides it is. Alizé's positioning is built on governance and security — but if the delivery process itself is ad hoc, the positioning is hollow.

**Platform/delivery collision:** D21 (manual orchestration first) is correct. But manual orchestration still requires someone to manually orchestrate. If Louis is the one doing it while managing dev priorities, the manual process becomes a bottleneck that delays both.

### Alternative Recommendation

**Phase 1 (Month 1): Louis delivers the pilot manually, documenting everything**
Louis runs the first pilot himself using direct tooling (no platform, no contractor). He acts as operator, delivery lead, and client success. Every step is documented as he goes: discovery questions, scoping criteria, implementation checklist, client communication templates, sign-off process.

**Phase 2 (Month 2): Hire or contract a delivery specialist — with explicit onboarding requirements**
The delivery person executes against the playbook Louis built in Month 1. Not to figure out how to deliver — to deliver against an existing process. Their first two weeks: read the documentation, shadow the first pilot debrief, observe a client check-in. No touching the second pilot until they can recite the process from memory.

**Role definition required before contract is signed:**
- Delivery specialist owns: implementation execution, client check-ins, weekly status reports, issue escalation
- Louis owns: platform oversight, strategic client decisions, new business, relationship with pilot client (not daily delivery)
- No overlap in decision rights without a documented escalation path

### What This Resolves
- Who owns the first client relationship (Louis)
- What the delivery playbook contains before Month 2 contractor arrives
- When formal role definitions are needed (before contracting, not after)

## Agent 2: Pilot Fee Strategist

### Assumptions Challenged

**A1: "At-cost" is a stable, calculable number**
The brief proposes €3,000–6,000 as the pilot setup range. But a range is not a price. Without a single, fixed, agreed figure, every client negotiation starts from a different baseline, and every scope discussion has no ceiling to bump against.

**A2: "Strict deliverables" resolves scope — it does not**
The pilot lists: one agent, one workflow, 2–4 tool connections, RAG knowledge base (up to 500 docs), guardrails, team training (one session), and 30-day monitoring. The brief also lists nine service components. These lists do not fully overlap. Nowhere is it written which of the nine are excluded from the pilot.

### The Case Against

**Financial risk — "at-cost" with two developers means zero margin on labor**
Louis and Gabin are the delivery team. If "at-cost" means their time is free, then every hour spent on a difficult pilot is uncompensated. A first pilot at a 20–30 person company may involve unfamiliar tools, slow client responses, custom integrations, and change requests. At cost, there is no margin.

**Scope risk — nine components, one pilot, no exclusion list**
A pilot priced at cost naturally invites clients to request "just a little more." Without an explicit scope ceiling, every added tool connection, every additional workflow variation, and every request for a second training session comes out of Alizé's margin.

**Timeline risk — "30-day monitoring included" has no day-one boundary**
The monitoring period is a deliverable, but it begins on go-live — not on contract signature. If the client is slow to approve access credentials, slow to provide documents for the RAG, or slow to schedule the training session, Alizé's timeline stretches and the monitoring period starts late.

### Alternative Recommendation

**Fixed pilot fee: €4,500 all-in**
Midpoint of the brief's own €3,000–6,000 range, stated as a single number, non-negotiable, with a signed scope document as the binding reference.

**What €4,500 covers:**
- One agent, one workflow
- 2–4 tool connections (MCP-based)
- RAG knowledge base (up to 200 documents — tightened from 500)
- Guardrails, permissions, audit logging
- One team training session (up to 90 minutes)
- 30-day monitoring, starting from go-live
- Written case study rights (including testimonial, anonymized metrics)

**What is explicitly excluded:**
- Additional workflows or agents
- Additional tool connections beyond 4
- Document ingestion beyond 200 documents
- Additional training sessions
- Ongoing managed service (Offer 3 begins only after pilot sign-off)
- Integration with tools requiring legal or compliance review

**Change triggers — any of the following activates a change order at €750/day pro-rated:**
- Client requests work outside the signed scope document
- Client delays access/resources beyond 5 business days from agreed timeline
- Client requests a new tool integration not in the original scope

**Exit criteria (agreed day one, written into scope):**
- Agent performs the defined workflow with >80% accuracy on test cases
- Client team can execute the workflow without Alizé intervention
- 30-day monitoring data shows measurable efficiency gain (hours saved or task completion rate)
- If none met: Alizé continues optimization at no additional cost for up to 15 days

### What This Resolves
- Pilot fee is fixed at €4,500 — no negotiation range
- Scope boundary is explicit and signed before work begins
- Change triggers are pre-agreed — eliminates scope creep arguments
- Exit criteria are defined — no ambiguity about pilot success

## Agent 3: Competitive Framing Strategist

### Assumptions Challenged

**A1: Naming competitors validates Alizé's market position**
This gets the logic backwards. In early outreach, the prospect has no context for why you're mentioning Agentova. It reads as insecurity — "we're the alternative to X" rather than "we solve Y problem." Mentioning competitors at first touch hands oxygen to the competitor. Agentova gets free brand reinforcement. That's their job, not yours.

**A2: Prospects at this stage want competitive comparison**
The target persona — founder/COO/head of ops at a 20–30 person company — is not in comparison-shopping mode. They are in problem-recognition mode. Leading with "unlike Agentova" or "unlike Microsoft" forces them to evaluate your claim before they've felt the pain you're addressing.

### The Case Against

**Risk 1 — Agentova users hear "you already have a solution"**
If a prospect is currently using Agentova and getting some value from it, your cold message that begins with "unlike Agentova..." has just told them their existing vendor is worth comparing against. You've elevated Agentova to the status of a serious player and planted the seed that comparison is relevant. You've opened a review cycle, not started a conversation.

**Risk 2 — Microsoft/HubSpot comparison sounds like a David vs. Goliath problem**
Naming Microsoft or HubSpot in first outreach implicitly frames yourself as a competitor to trillion-dollar companies. Neither is the tone for a €1,500/month managed service sale to a 30-person company.

**Risk 3 — Competitor mentions are conversation killers, not starters**
Cold outreach has one job: get the prospect to feel seen. "I noticed your company does X, we help companies like yours solve Y" is a conversation. "We're better than Agentova" is a claim that triggers immediate skepticism.

### Alternative Recommendation

**Lead with the problem. Let the differentiation be implicit.**

Instead of: "Unlike Agentova, which is self-serve, we manage everything for you."

Say: "Most companies we work with tried building something in-house or using a generic tool, and the problem wasn't the AI — it was that nobody had time to maintain it, configure it, or make sure it actually connected to their real workflow. We do that part."

**Concrete framing for first outreach message:**
> "Hi [Name], I work with operations leads at [industry] companies in the 20–100 person range — the kind of companies that have already tried ChatGPT or a workflow tool, but find the gap between 'having AI' and 'AI that actually does the work' is bigger than expected. We manage that gap end-to-end: we build the agent, connect it to your tools, and run it for you — so your team just sees the results."

**Three rules for competitive framing:**
1. Never name a competitor in the first message. If they ask in the first meeting, then you can compare — but only if the prospect raises it first.
2. Frame differentiation around the *problem generic tools create* (maintenance, config, connection) rather than the competitor's specific weakness.
3. In second outreach or follow-up: "The companies we see getting the most value are the ones that tried a self-service tool first and realized it wasn't enough" — this implicitly references competitors without naming them.

### What This Resolves
- First outreach message language — no competitor names
- Meeting preparation: if prospect asks about competitors, a prepared comparison response
- Competitive intelligence: track when prospects mention competitors unprompted — that's signal
