# Pulse 65 — Commercial Architecture Debate

## Assumption Challenged

**"The project-first commercial model (€3-5K setup → proof period → retainer) is the right structure for Alizé Year 1."**

This is D219's framing: €3-5K setup → €1,200-1,500/mo proof period → €2,000-2,500/mo earned managed service. The assumption challenged is not the price points — those have been debated extensively — but the **structural architecture**: a multi-stage commitment sequence starting with a non-trivial buyer payment before trust is established, for an unknown vendor with zero external commercial activity in 91+ days.

---

## Arguments Against

### Challenge 1: The multi-stage structure requires the buyer to decide five times before Alizé has decided anything

The standard project-first flow contains: (1) agree to €3-5K setup, (2) agree to 1-3 month proof period commitment, (3) agree proof period results justify continuation, (4) agree to monthly retainer at €1,200-1,500, (5) agree to transition to €2,000-2,500 earned managed service. That's five buyer decisions stacked on each other, each gated on the previous.

French PME decision-making at 20-200 employees involves the DG directly. DGs at this size do not make purchasing decisions in stages — they make them once, based on a single convincing answer to "why this, why now, why you." The stage-gate model is an enterprise SaaS construct borrowed from companies with procurement departments. French PMEs of 20-50 employees have one person who either trusts you or doesn't.

Every gate between initial contact and recurring revenue is a drop-off point. For a known vendor with a reference client, this works — the buyer is being cautious for good reason. For an unknown vendor with zero social proof, each gate is an opportunity to walk away. The model assumes the buyer is already convinced and just needs to verify in stages. The opposite is true: the buyer needs to be convinced quickly or they move on.

**Specific failure mode in French PME context:** A French DG receives an Alizé outreach message. The conversation goes: "We'd start with a €3,000-5,000 setup, then run a 2-month proof period at €1,200/month, and if it works, graduate to a €2,000/month retainer." Her response is not "let me think about the proof period pricing" — it's "I need to think about whether I trust you with my invoice processing at all." The multi-stage structure is a solution to a problem the buyer doesn't have.

---

### Challenge 2: "Proof period → earned retainer" inverts the trust economy for a new vendor

The logic of "proof period → earned retainer" is vendor-protective in intent but trust-inverting in practice:

1. Alizé charges the client to prove itself (setup fee + proof period payment)
2. Client pays while Alizé performs
3. If Alizé performs, the client "earns" the right to continue at a higher retainer

This is backwards from how trust operates in French B2B at the PME level. The party with less credibility bears more of the upfront risk. This is not how French business relationships start.

The correct trust sequence for an unknown vendor: vendor demonstrates first (free prototype, free diagnostic, free working artifact) → client observes value → client pays for what they've already seen working → retainer follows only after the client has decided to continue.

**French market specific:** French business relationships are built on "faire ses preuves" (proving oneself). A new supplier to a French PME is expected to demonstrate competence before invoicing. The current model inverts this: it invoices before demonstration.

---

### Challenge 3: The model is designed against Louis's credibility ceiling, not for it

D84 established that Louis cannot credibly close €7,500+ deals without a senior commercial co-founder. D184 clarified that €2,000 is NOT validated as within his credibility ceiling either. The credibility ceiling analysis has been conducted for price floors but not for the **structural credibility required to ask a stranger for a multi-stage commitment**.

The €3-5K setup fee is not just a price — it's a signal: "We are a company that charges setup fees." The question is whether Louis, in his current commercial position (student/intern/founder with no commercial track record, no case studies, no public references), can credibly ask a French DG to pay €3-5K upfront.

The multi-stage model requires Louis to be persuasive about five sequential commitments. None of these are executable by a solo technical founder with no sales training and no commercial track record. The model's complexity — not just its price points — is mismatched to Louis's current capabilities.

**Evidence from 91+ days of zero commercial action:** Louis has not made a single external commercial outreach in 91+ days. The 91-day paralysis is not a motivation problem — it's a structural problem. The model is too complex to execute without a commercial layer.

---

### Challenge 4: The €2,000-2,500/month retainer target is unsupported by unit economics and market validation

D167 noted that the €2,000/month retainer covers 25% of cost-plus minimum (€7,963/month total). If the managed service cannot be priced at a level that covers delivery costs while remaining sellable, the commercial model itself is unworkable regardless of payment structure.

The market validation problem: P57-D1 states "all commercial models are theoretical without market validation." The project-first model was designed in research, debated in research, and revised in research — but never presented to a single prospect.

**The specific contradiction:** D219 says the model is "structurally broken: senior co-founder OR downmarket OR channel partner required." The current plan is none of these. This isn't a pricing revision problem — it's an acknowledgment that the structural model requires a capability that hasn't been acquired.

---

### Challenge 5: The "project-first" framing mispositions Alizé as a vendor rather than a partner

The D226 hero sentence — "We take over your invoice processing, CRM updates, and client FAQ responses — the 15 hours a week of repetitive data work that your senior staff are doing manually — and run it so you never have to think about it again" — promises operational ownership transfer.

The project-first commercial model says the opposite: "first you pay us to set up, then you pay us to prove ourselves, then IF we earn it, we take over." A buyer who hears D226's promise and then receives D219's commercial structure experiences cognitive dissonance. The promise implies embedded partnership. The structure implies cautious vendor onboarding.

---

## Arguments For (Steel-Man)

**1. Cash flow protection:** Without a setup fee, Alizé funds delivery out of pocket for 1-3 months. The setup fee provides necessary cash flow to cover Louis's time and infrastructure during the proof period.

**2. Filtering for serious buyers:** A non-trivial setup fee (€3-5K) filters for prospects with genuine intent, not just curiosity. Qualifying via payment commitment is more efficient than qualifying via discovery calls with prospects who will never convert.

**3. French ESN industry precedent:** French IT services companies (ESNs) routinely use setup/project phases before transitioning to ongoing managed service. This is a recognized commercial structure that clients understand and expect.

**4. Margin recovery if the proof period fails:** If Alizé delivers poor results, the setup fee partially compensates for the delivered work.

**5. Psychological commitment after payment:** Behavioral economics (Cialdini's commitment principle) shows that people who pay are more likely to follow through. A paid setup fee may improve proof period outcomes.

**Verdict on steel-man:** The arguments are valid in a world where: (a) Alizé has established credibility, (b) Louis has a commercial layer, (c) the market expects this structure, and (d) cash flow is the binding constraint. None of these conditions currently exist. The steel-man case describes a mature version of Alizé, not the Year 1 version.

---

## Decision

**KILL the project-first commercial model as currently structured.**

The multi-stage payment structure is designed for a company that has: established credibility, a commercial sales team, validated pricing, and proven delivery. Alizé has none of these.

**Recommended replacement approach — outcome-first, not payment-first:**

1. **Free demonstration:** Build a working prototype of the specific workflow for the specific prospect. Show it running. No charge, no commitment.
2. **Priced outcome engagement:** After the prospect sees the working agent doing their job, price a specific engagement: "We run this for you at €X/month, with these specific deliverables and this specific exit criteria." One price, one conversation, one decision.
3. **No staging:** The prospect either wants the ongoing service or they don't. Staging into "setup → proof → retainer" creates three opportunities to say no instead of one clear yes-or-no.

**The commercial model should match the positioning:** If D226 says "we run it so you never have to think about it again," the commercial model should be: "Here's the agent running your invoice processing. It works. Do you want us to continue running it? It's €X/month."
