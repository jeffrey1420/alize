## What Was Debated

### Topic 1: Is Alizé's Service Model the Problem?

**Assumption challenged:** "The constraint is Louis's time, not the model. Adding delivery partners will solve the scaling problem."

**Verdict: The assumption is partially right but fundamentally misleading. The model is not a stepping stone to something better — the service IS the product.**

**Key findings:**

- At 3-4 pilots in Year 1, Louis doesn't have a time problem — he has a *demand* problem. 25 hrs/week × 48 weeks = 1,200 hours/year. 3 pilots × 150 hours = 450 hours. Louis uses at most 50% of his Alizé capacity.
- **He does not need delivery partners. He needs clients.**
- Model yields at 3/6/12 clients are real: Year 1 €43-90K revenue, Year 2 €86-180K, Year 3 €172-360K. Not venture scale but not nothing.
- **The ceiling is execution risk, not structural.** Louis's credibility ceiling (D84: €7,500+) doesn't apply to expert-comptable deals at €1,200-2,500/month. That's a tooling budget conversation, not a credibility test.
- **Adding delivery partners now makes things worse.** Margin markup (20-30%), coordination overhead, converting a high-margin solo practice into a logistics business.
- **The right time to add partners is when turning down work, not before having work.**

**Assumption from existing research found wrong:** "The constraint is Louis's time, not the model" misidentifies the bottleneck. At Year 1 scale, Louis's time is not the constraint — demand is. The service model is not a delivery mechanism for something better.

### Topic 2: Are Expert-Comptables the Right First Vertical?

**Assumption challenged:** "Expert-comptables are the right first vertical because the governance gap makes Copilot disqualifying."

**Verdict: The assumption is partially wrong — the governance gap exists but is not disqualifying, and without it the vertical has no clear moat.**

**Key findings:**

- **D127 already contradicted the premise.** D127 reframed the governance gap as "overstated for 70% of ICP." If 70% of accounting firms can use Copilot without governance concerns, Copilot is not disqualifying for the majority. You're left arguing Alizé is better for 30% of firms — a segment-of-one problem.
- **Louis has zero confirmed expert-comptable network contacts.** U123 is explicitly unresolved. The entire GTM (D120: warm outreach to Louis's network) has no foundation in this vertical. Cold outreach to 13,000 dispersed firms is not a startup-friendly motion.
- **The accounting software integration surface is a complication, not an advantage.** Pennylane raised €40M+ and is actively building AI. Cegid is a €2B+ enterprise company. These incumbents already own the workflow data Alizé wants to automate. "You can't out-AI a platform that already has the data."
- **Regulatory urgency is a double-edged sword.** Risk-averse firms wait for certified solutions. Their existing vendors will offer "compliant AI" before third parties.
- **D126's vertical rationale collapses without the governance gap argument.** The other two arguments (regulatory urgency, repetitive workflows) are downstream justifications that only hold if Copilot is disqualifying.

**Assumption from existing research found wrong:** The governance gap as a vertical-wide moat is overstated. D127 already said so. Without this differentiator, expert-comptables require a network Louis doesn't have, compete against incumbents with the data, and face regulatory risk aversion that slows adoption.

### Topic 3: Is the Binary Choice a Prerequisite to Execution?

**Assumption challenged:** "Louis needs to decide between 'startup' and 'side-project' before meaningful execution can begin."

**Verdict: The assumption is backwards. The binary choice is an avoidance mechanism, not a blocker. Execution must precede resolution, not follow it.**

**Key findings:**

- **The binary choice has blocked Louis for 38+ pulses without unblocking him.** It has given him permission to delay. "I can't execute until I decide" is procrastination with extra steps.
- **The real decision Louis is avoiding is not "which path" but "do I have what it takes."** Can I close deals? Can I be the commercial leader? These are the questions the binary framing lets him avoid.
- **There is a third path: execution without identity resolution.** Louis can interview customers, build an MVP, approach partners — all without declaring "I am now a startup founder." He can remain in ambiguity while gathering data that makes the choice obvious.
- **What happens if Louis just starts?** Customers don't want it → saved 6 months. Customers DO want it → choice becomes self-evident. The work itself reveals commitment level.
- **The binary choice was never the missing key.** Execution is the key. The choice will *emerge from* execution, not precede it.

**Assumption from existing research found wrong:** "Decide before executing" treats commitment as a prerequisite to action. This inverts causality. Louis doesn't need more information to decide — he needs to execute to generate the information that makes the decision obvious.

## The Pattern Across 39 Pulses

Three patterns are now unmistakable:

1. **The service model is viable but demand-constrained.** The research keeps asking "can the model scale" when the real question is "can Louis generate enough demand at the right price point." The model yields are real. The demand generation is the unknown.
2. **The expert-comptable vertical was chosen on a premise D127 already contradicted.** The governance gap that was supposed to differentiate expert-comptables from Copilot was reframed as "overstated for 70% of ICP" in the same research cycle. The vertical needs a hard re-evaluation against Louis's actual network, not against a moat that may not exist.
3. **The binary choice has been a 39-pulse delay tactic.** Louis has received the same advice six ways: decide, execute, stop researching. The choice will emerge from the work — not precede it.
